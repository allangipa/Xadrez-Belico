Operation Market Garden

17 to 25 September 1944 · The corridor from Eindhoven to Arnhem, in the provinces of North Brabant and Gelderland, the Netherlands, by way of Son, Veghel, Grave and Nijmegen

On 17 September 1944, about 35,000 American, British and Polish paratroopers and glider troops come down over the Netherlands to seize, in one stroke, the bridges along a road of about 100 km — from Eindhoven to Arnhem, over canals, the Maas, the Waal and the Rhine. Along that road, a British armored corps was to reach the Rhine in two days, four at most, and open the way into Germany.

Nine days later, the survivors of the division that was waiting at Arnhem cross the river back, at night, in boats. The question on this page is not whether the plan was bold — it was. It is why it stopped exactly before the last bridge, and why the answer that circulates most, that nobody knew about the German tanks, is the least complete.

Move 1The plan: a chain of bridges, on a single road

In September 1944, after Normandy, the Allied armies had swept the Germans out of almost all of France and Belgium, and the Allied command believed the German army was falling apart. Montgomery was asking for a single, full-blooded thrust across the Rhine. On 10 September Eisenhower approved a version of it, with limited priority: Market Garden.

The design was simple to state. Three airborne divisions and a Polish brigade would seize the bridges along a road that ran from the Belgian border to Arnhem, about 100 km to the north: the US 101st at Son and Veghel, the 82nd at Grave and Nijmegen, the British 1st, the farthest away, at Arnhem. Up the road, XXX Corps would advance in two days, four at most. The timetable had Eindhoven by the late afternoon of the first day and Arnhem at 15:00 on the third.

On the board, this is a chain in series. Every bridge had to fall intact, in order and on time, because the whole armored corps and the bridging equipment came along a two-lane highway, partly raised above the polder, with ditches and dikes on either side. There was no parallel road. A delay at any link passed on to all the following ones, and the last link, Arnhem, was held by the division that depended on all of them.

Move 2The farthest piece, delivered in installments and far from the target

The first constraint was one of means. The available aircraft could carry about 60% of the force in a single lift, and the troops were delivered over three days. The 1st Airborne Division landed on 17 September with half its strength, and of that half only the 1st Parachute Brigade could march on the bridge: the rest had to guard the landing zones for the next day's lift.

The second was one of place. The zones lay on the heaths west of Oosterbeek, 8 to 13 km from the bridge, depending on the source. North of the city was the anti-aircraft defense of Deelen airfield; south of the bridge, the only flat ground, the Malburgsche polder, was small for the gliders of a brigade and lay next to an anti-aircraft battery. After the war Urquhart himself told the British official historian that the air force, rightly, had not wanted to fly into the defended zone. Martin Middlebrook calls the refusal of night drops, of two lifts on the first day and of a coup de main on the bridge fundamental errors; John Frost called the distance a glaring obstacle.

On the first day, two of the three columns were held up by improvised German units west of Arnhem. Only Frost's 2nd Battalion, along the riverside road, reached the bridge at dusk and took its northern end. The railway bridge at Oosterbeek was blown up as the British reached it. And at a distance of 13 km, the radios between the bridge and division headquarters could not reach each other: John Greenacre's study points to the distance between units, greater than the range of the sets, as the main cause.

Move 3The first link breaks at Son, the second jams at Nijmegen

On 17 September, the bridge at Son over the Wilhelmina Canal was blown up by the Germans as the 101st reached it. XXX Corps, which had set off at 14:35 after an artillery barrage, was ambushed just past the border and halted for the night at Valkenswaard, without reaching Eindhoven. It got there on the afternoon of the 18th, a day late, and the engineers put up a Bailey bridge at Son during the night.

Farther north, the 82nd took Grave and a bridge over the Maas–Waal Canal, but concentrated its effort on the Groesbeek heights, to block an attack coming from the Reichswald forest, in Germany, and only sent troops against the Nijmegen bridge at night. Browning agreed with that priority. When the attack came, units of the 10th SS Panzer Division were already arriving, and the bridge stayed German. The US official history counts four attempts against it before the one that succeeded.

On 19 September XXX Corps met the 82nd at Grave and the delay seemed to have been made up. But the assault crossing the plan provided for in case a bridge was not taken depended on boats that were far back, on the same road. They arrived on the afternoon of the 20th. At around 15:00, the 3rd Battalion of the 504th Parachute Infantry Regiment crossed the Waal in daylight, in 26 canvas boats, under fire; half the boats survived the first trip. In the early evening, Guards tanks crossed the road bridge. The bridge that was supposed to fall on the first day fell on the fourth.

Move 4The night of 20 September: the most debated move

On the far side of the Waal, the tanks stopped. They resumed the advance about 18 hours later, at noon on the 21st, and soon ran into a German line at Ressen, less than 5 km to the north. In Arnhem, 11 km away, Frost's group was still holding out that night.

It is the scene most often repeated as the explanation, and it deserves to be weighed carefully. Against the halt there is the commander of the 10th SS, Heinz Harmel, who decades later told Robert Kershaw that if those tanks had kept going, it would have been all over for the Germans. In its favor there is the darkness, only five tanks on the other side, hidden anti-tank guns, the city of Nijmegen still being fought over behind them, and what Harmel did not know: three Tiger tanks and two infantry companies were already coming down from Arnhem toward the village of Lent.

And there is the clock. The Arnhem bridge fell in the early hours of the 21st. Even a night advance would have had to cover 11 km of road raised above the polder — terrain that the commander of the Guards Armoured Division, on seeing it, described as the most unsuitable for tanks that could be imagined. The halt of 20 September was the last delay, not the decisive one. The decisive one came earlier, at Son and at the Nijmegen bridge.

Move 5What the opponent did with the time it was given

Allied intelligence knew there was German armor around Arnhem. Ultra located the 9th and 10th SS Panzer Divisions there; Eisenhower's chief of staff, Walter Bedell Smith, took the concern to Montgomery on 15 September and was brushed aside. Browning's intelligence officer, Major Brian Urquhart, showed reports from the Dutch resistance and aerial photographs, and was sent on leave for nervous strain and exhaustion. Montgomery later wrote that the Allies had been wrong to assume that corps could not fight effectively.

But the two divisions were depleted and almost without tanks, and no German tank fought on the first day. What weighed was something else. Field Marshal Walter Model, commander of Army Group B, was staying at a hotel in Oosterbeek when the British landed a few kilometers away. He left in a hurry, assessed the situation and sent to Arnhem all the armor he could find, while German officers turned leftovers — schools, reserve battalions, air force and navy men — into combat units within hours. John Hackett, a British brigade commander, said after the war that a German headquarters could make a cohesive unit out of stragglers in a time the Allies thought impossible.

The weather did the rest. From 18 September on, seven of eight days had bad weather: the second lift was delayed by hours, the Polish brigade arrived two days later than planned, and almost all the supplies dropped north of the Rhine fell into German hands. In a plan with slack, that would have been a setback. In a plan in series, every hour lost was charged at the last bridge.

Move 6Oosterbeek and the way back across the river

Unable to reach the bridge, the 1st Airborne Division closed itself into a perimeter at Oosterbeek, with about 3,600 men, backed against the Rhine. The Poles finally dropped on 21 September, at Driel, on the south bank, and found that the ferry no longer existed; the improvised night crossings of the following days put only a few hundred men on the other side. On 24 September, an attempt by a British battalion ended with about 200 of 315 men captured.

On the night of the 25th to the 26th, in the rain, British and Canadian engineers brought back in small boats 2,398 men: 1,741 from the division, 422 glider pilots, 160 Poles and 75 from the Dorset battalion. About 300 were left on the north bank when day broke. Of the troops that landed at Arnhem, 1,485 died and about 6,400 were taken prisoner.

After the defeat, Montgomery and Browning blamed the Polish brigade and Sosabowski was removed. Historians such as Carlo D'Este treat the accusation as scapegoating, and the Netherlands decorated the brigade in 2006. Montgomery, for his part, called Market Garden 90% successful. Chester Wilmot answered that the sum only adds up if success is measured by the number of bridges taken.

VerdictWhy it ended the way it did

Market Garden did not fail because of one mistake. It failed because it was a chain of bets in series — every bridge intact, in order, on time, along a single road — and because the piece that depended on all of them, at Arnhem, was delivered in three installments, kilometers from the target. Discarded intelligence, radios out of range, distant landing zones, the Nijmegen bridge taken on the fourth day and bad weather are not rival explanations: they are the same flaw seen from different places. In a plan in series, failures do not add up, they multiply.

In chess terms, it is a six-move combination that only works if the opponent does not answer any of them. The opponent answered on the first: it blew the bridge at Son. Model, by chance a few kilometers from the landing zones, answered all the others. The US official history sums it up: by the unrelenting logic of war, Market Garden was a failure.

And the phrase that gave the defeat its name may never have been said. "A bridge too far" appears in writing in 1958, in General Urquhart's memoirs, attributed to Browning; the historian Antony Beevor doubts that Browning said it. It also points to the wrong bridge: the one that jammed the clock was Nijmegen.

MythsWhat is told and what the record shows

  • What is told

    Browning warned Montgomery that Arnhem was a bridge too far.

    What the record shows

    The phrase appears in writing in 1958, in General Roy Urquhart's memoirs, and was popularized by Cornelius Ryan's book in 1974 and by the 1977 film. Antony Beevor disputes it: Browning supported the operation, especially after getting more resources, and does not seem to have met Montgomery that day.

  • What is told

    The operation failed because nobody knew there were SS armored divisions at Arnhem.

    What the record shows

    It was known. Ultra located the 9th and 10th SS Panzer Divisions; Bedell Smith warned Montgomery on 15 September, and Major Brian Urquhart warned Browning. The warning was dismissed. But the two divisions were depleted and almost without tanks: what weighed was Model's quick reaction and the German ability to improvise units out of leftovers.

  • What is told

    The British radios failed because they came with the wrong crystals.

    What the record shows

    Major John Greenacre's study points to the distance between units, greater than the range of the sets, as the main cause. The wrong crystals affected two sets, those of the American air support team.

  • What is told

    XXX Corps stopped at Nijmegen and left the Arnhem paratroopers to die.

    What the record shows

    The halt on the night of 20 September happened and lasted about 18 hours, with only five tanks on the other side of the Waal, hidden anti-tank guns and three Tigers coming down from Arnhem. The Arnhem bridge fell in the early hours of the next day. The decisive delays came earlier: the bridge at Son, blown up on 17 September, and the Nijmegen bridge, which was supposed to fall on the first day and fell on the fourth.

QuestionsFrequently asked questions

What was Operation Market Garden?

It was the Allied operation of 17 to 25 September 1944 in the Netherlands: about 35,000 airborne troops would seize the bridges along a road of about 100 km, from Eindhoven to Arnhem, so that a British armored corps could reach the Rhine in 2 days, 4 at most.

Was Operation Market Garden a failure?

Yes, in the objective that gave meaning to the others: the Rhine crossing at Arnhem failed. Eindhoven and Nijmegen were liberated and a salient of about 105 km was opened, and Montgomery spoke of 90% success, but the US official history sums it up as a failure.

Why did Market Garden fail?

It was a chain of bridges in series on a single road, and every delay passed on to the next bridge. The bridge at Son was blown up on the first day, the one at Nijmegen only fell on the fourth, and the Arnhem division was delivered over three days, kilometers from the target, against Model's quick reaction and bad weather.

What were the casualties of Operation Market Garden?

Of the troops that landed at Arnhem, 1,485 died and about 6,400 were taken prisoner. Allied casualties for the whole operation range from 15,326 to 17,200, depending on the source; German ones, from 3,300 in the official German count to more than 13,000 in historians' estimates.

Who said “a bridge too far”?

The phrase is attributed to General Frederick Browning and appears in writing in 1958, in General Roy Urquhart's memoirs. The historian Antony Beevor doubts that Browning said it, because he supported the operation.
