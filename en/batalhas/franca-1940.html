Episode 05 · World War II, 1940

The Fall of France

10 May to 25 June 1940 · France, Belgium, the Netherlands and Luxembourg; the point of decision at Sedan, on the Meuse River, Ardennes department, France

If there was no new doctrine in September 1939, what exactly did Germany apply in France in May 1940?

On 10 May 1940, when Germany attacks in the west, the Allies have 13,974 guns against 7,378 German ones. France alone has between 3,383 and 4,071 tanks; Germany goes in with 2,445. Six weeks later, France is out of the war.

The Polish campaign showed that, in 1939, there was no doctrine called Blitzkrieg: the name came from the newspapers. This page carries the question forward. If it was not a doctrine, what was applied eight months later? The answer fits in one distinction: a doctrine is taught to an entire army and serves for any war; a plan serves for one war, against one enemy, on one terrain.

Move 1The board: the side that lost had more pieces

On 10 May 1940 Germany had 141 divisions; the Allies, 135. Men, 3,300,000 on each side. So far, a draw. The next numbers are not a draw: the Allies had 13,974 guns against 7,378 German ones — almost twice as many — and France alone had between 3,383 and 4,071 tanks, against 2,445 for all of Germany.

The Germans' only clear material advantage was in the air: 5,638 aircraft against fewer than 2,935. The campaign that would end France's part in the war in six weeks was won by an army with fewer tanks and little more than half the opponent's artillery. If the answer is not in the matériel, it is somewhere else on the board.

Move 2The rejected opening: the 1914 plan, an airplane and a sidelined general

The first directive for the west, Aufmarschanweisung N°1 (Fall Gelb, Case Yellow), was drawn up by Franz Halder, chief of the general staff. It pushed the Allies through central Belgium to the Somme River — similar to the 1914 campaign. Pushing is not encircling: an army that is pushed falls back and reorganizes; an army that is encircled is finished. The original plan gained ground, not the war.

On 10 January 1940 a German airplane carrying documents of the plan comes down in Belgium. It is chance, and should be read as chance. The incident forces a revision, and Halder rewrites the directive.

The idea that would end up adopted was Erich von Manstein's: go in from the south, through the Ardennes, cross the Meuse at Sedan, turn west and race for the sea. The high command rejected it; Halder and the commander-in-chief Walther von Brauchitsch called it "too bold". At the end of January, Halder had Manstein promoted to command XXXVIII Corps in eastern Germany, away from the planning.

The idea survived by a side route. On 2 February, Hitler heard of it from Rudolf Schmundt; on the 13th, he ordered the plan changed; on the 17th, he received Manstein in person. On 24 February 1940, Halder rewrote the directive as Aufmarschanweisung N°4, now with the main axis through the Ardennes — two and a half months before the attack.

Move 3The only thing truly new: where the pieces were placed

It was not the tank: both sides had tanks, and the Allies had more. It was not the airplane, which had already flown over Poland. It was not the word, which the newspapers had already invented. The novelty of May 1940 was concentration.

Panzergruppe von Kleist crosses the Ardennes with 134,000 men and 1,222 tanks, in five armored divisions — half of the German tanks on a single axis. The Allies had more tanks and spread them along the whole front. The Germans had fewer and piled half of them into a forest.

And the price of that concentration was an enormous vulnerability. According to the French Wikipedia, citing Mary (2009) and Frieser (2003), the group had only four reserved routes to enter Luxembourg, the Panzerstraßen numbered one to four. Half of Germany's armor on four forest roads is a valuable piece left exposed: the move only works if the opponent does not look at it.

Move 4The Allied move: Dyle, Breda and the general who warned

For the German plan to work, the Allies had to go north. They did. On 9 November 1939, Maurice Gamelin adopted the Dyle Plan: at the first sign of invasion, the best Allied armies would advance into Belgium and hold there. On 12 March 1940 he added the Breda variant: the French 7th Army would advance as far as the Dutch city of Breda, to link up with the Dutch — 175 km to cover, against Germans who were 90 km from the same place.

On 10 May, into Belgium and the Netherlands go the 7th Army (Giraud), the British Expeditionary Force (Gort) with 9 divisions, the French 1st Army (Blanchard) with 10 and the 9th Army (Corap), the last south of Namur, along the Meuse. With them go some of the best divisions of the General Reserve, leaving "few reserves from the Rhine to the Channel".

General Alphonse Georges had warned against sending the bulk of the French mobile forces against an attack on the northern flank that might be a diversion for a German attack through the center. He described the blow before it happened, and the reserve went north anyway. Meanwhile, French intelligence followed the wrong movement: it discovered the transfer of German divisions from the Saar to north of the Moselle, and did not detect the redeployment from the Dutch border to the Eifel–Moselle area, where the blow would come from.

Move 5Sedan, 13 May: the bombing that broke the telephone

At Sedan, the French defense was the 55th Infantry Division, under General Henri Lafontaine: a Category B division of reservists, most of them over thirty, poorly trained for combat because it had been assigned to construction work. Beside it, the 71st Division was still moving into the line. In all, 20,000 men and 174 artillery pieces. Across the river, the 1st, 2nd and 10th Panzer Divisions and the Großdeutschland Regiment: 60,000 men, 41,000 vehicles, 788 tanks and 141 guns.

From 08:00 to 16:00 on 13 May, the Luftwaffe pounds Sedan: about 3,940 bomber sorties by nine Kampfgeschwader, at the cost of six aircraft. And then the fact that reverses the reading: not one of the bunkers was destroyed by a direct hit, and the bombing caused 56 French casualties.

The damage was indirect. The telecommunication cables broke and the division was left without communications; the gunners abandoned their positions before the ground attack began. The bombing did not break the concrete. It broke the link between the pieces and the will of those who worked them.

Move 6Bulson, 19:00: panic over tanks that had not yet crossed

At around 19:00 on 13 May, a French artillery observer's report is passed on wrongly. A rumor spreads that German tanks are approaching the village of Bulson, south of Sedan. The 55th Division abandons its positions, and by the time the mistake is noticed the artillery and infantry have already left their heavy equipment behind.

The first German tank crossed the Meuse at 07:20 on 14 May, more than twelve hours later. The panic that undid the defense of Sedan was over tanks that had not yet crossed the river: the position was taken by infantry crossing in boats, against a line that fell apart because of a wrong piece of information.

The French had the means to respond. X Corps was ordered to counterattack at 05:00 on 14 May, with about 300 tanks in the area, 138 of them combat tanks. The French armor only reached the Bulson ridge seventeen hours after the order, and the German vanguard got there first, by a few minutes. The English Wikipedia records the assessment that a quick counter-thrust by just two infantry regiments and two tank battalions would have "plunged the Germans into crisis". The means were there; the reaction time was not.

Move 7The final score

The campaign runs from 10 May to 25 June 1940, and the armistice is signed on 22 June, at Compiègne. On the German side, 27,074 killed, 111,034 wounded, 18,384 missing and between 795 and 822 tanks lost. On the Allied side, 73,000 killed, 240,000 wounded, 15,000 missing — and 1,756,000 prisoners.

It was a fast, large and complete victory. France fought: the 73,000 Allied dead in six weeks say so. The question was never whether the plan worked, but what exactly worked.

VerdictWhy it ended the way it did

What Germany applied in May 1940 was not a doctrine: it was a specific plan, for one enemy and one terrain, rejected by the high command itself, whose author was removed from the planning, and adopted only on 24 February 1940 through intervention from outside the planning chain. The advantage was not in the matériel — the Allies had more tanks and almost twice as many guns — but in where the pieces were placed: half of the German tanks on a single axis, against the point where the opponent had reservist divisions and no reserve behind them.

The plan depended on an Allied move, and the move came: the best French mobile forces rushed north, against Alphonse Georges's warning. And the decision at Sedan came from something no plan foresees — a division without telephones abandoning intact positions because of tanks that were more than twelve hours away, and a counterattack that arrived minutes after the opponent.

None of this was luck: concentrating half the armor, crossing the Ardennes and crossing the Meuse is execution of the highest level. But, in chess terms, it was not a school of play applied from memory. It was home preparation against a specific opponent, which the player decided to use in that game — and which his own coach had rejected.

MythsWhat is told and what the record shows

  • What is told

    In May 1940 Germany applied a doctrine called Blitzkrieg.

    What the record shows

    What was applied was a plan, Manstein's, which the German high command rejected as "too bold" and only adopted on 24 February 1940, after Hitler stepped in. Karl-Heinz Frieser argues that the thinking now called Blitzkrieg only took shape after this campaign: a consequence of victory, not its cause.

  • What is told

    France fell to a materially superior war machine.

    What the record shows

    On 10 May 1940 the Allies had 13,974 guns against 7,378 German ones, and France alone had between 3,383 and 4,071 tanks against 2,445 German ones. Germany's only clear material superiority was in the air. The difference lay in concentration: 1,222 tanks in a single group, on a single axis.

  • What is told

    At Sedan, the Luftwaffe pulverized the French defenses.

    What the record shows

    Eight hours of bombing, about 3,940 sorties, and not one bunker destroyed by a direct hit; 56 French casualties. The damage was to the communication cables and to morale: the gunners abandoned their positions, and that same night the 55th Division fell back because of a rumor about tanks that would only cross the Meuse at 07:20 the next day.

QuestionsFrequently asked questions

How long did the Battle of France last?

About six weeks: Germany attacked in the west on 10 May 1940, France signed the armistice on 22 June, at Compiègne, and the campaign ended on 25 June.

Why did France fall in 1940?

Not for lack of matériel, but because of where the pieces were placed. Germany concentrated half of its tanks on a single axis, through the Ardennes to Sedan, while the best Allied mobile forces advanced north, into Belgium. At Sedan, the French defense fell apart before the first tank crossed the Meuse.

Did Germany have more tanks than France in 1940?

No. Germany went in with 2,445 tanks; France alone had between 3,383 and 4,071, depending on the count. The Allies also had 13,974 guns against 7,378 German ones; Germany's only clear material superiority was in the air.

How many soldiers died in the Battle of France?

On the German side, 27,074 killed, 111,034 wounded and 18,384 missing. On the Allied side, 73,000 killed, 240,000 wounded and 15,000 missing, plus 1,756,000 prisoners.

Who planned the German attack in the Battle of France?

Erich von Manstein, with the idea of crossing the Ardennes, crossing the Meuse at Sedan and racing for the sea. The high command rejected it as too bold; after Hitler stepped in, Franz Halder rewrote it as a directive on 24 February 1940.
