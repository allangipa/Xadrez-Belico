The Ardennes

16 December 1944 to 25 January 1945 · The Ardennes, Belgium and Luxembourg — from Monschau and the Elsenborn Ridge, in the north, to Echternach, in the south, and as far as Celles, near Dinant, by the Meuse River

On 16 September 1944, Hitler interrupted a meeting at his headquarters to announce a counterattack: out of the Ardennes, with Antwerp as the objective. The declared goal was another Dunkirk, and the route was the same as in 1940 — the forest through which German armor had passed to cut France in two. On 16 December, more than 200,000 men attacked four American divisions spread across almost 140 km of front.

The offensive broke the line, encircled Bastogne and came within less than 10 km of the Meuse. And it stopped there. This page follows the game to answer why the move that had worked once did not work again — and the answer lies neither in fuel alone nor in the clearing sky alone, which are the explanations that circulate most.

Move 1The 1940 move, copied on purpose

In September 1944 Germany was retreating on both fronts. Hitler chose the place where he had won four years earlier: the Ardennes, the forest between Belgium and Luxembourg through which German armor had passed in May 1940. The U.S. Army's official historian, Hugh M. Cole, records that Hitler's proposals that autumn bore more than a casual resemblance to those before the 1940 offensive, and that he believed he could repeat the result. The declared goal was another Dunkirk.

The target was Antwerp, the great Allied port, which had received its first convoy only on 28 November. Taking it would cut off the British and Canadians in the north from the Americans in the south. The two field marshals charged with carrying out the plan thought the target too big. Walter Model summed it up, according to Cole: the plan did not have a leg to stand on. He and Rundstedt preferred a smaller solution, an encirclement east of the Meuse. Hitler kept Antwerp.

The plan required three things that in 1940 had come for free: break the line on the first day, reach the Meuse in three or four, and do it all under bad weather, which would stand in for the air force Germany no longer had. Cole records that the planners were counting on up to ten days of overcast skies.

Move 2The same board, different pieces

The campaign brochure of the U.S. Army Center of Military History compares the two crossings coolly. Earlier offensives through the Ardennes had followed the main roads, in good weather, against light screens that were easily brushed aside. In 1940, German armor, barely contested, needed three days to cross the easiest ground in the south of the forest, on dry roads. Cole calls that crossing little more than a road march.

In 1944 the terrain was the same and the pieces were not. There were ten all-weather roads crossing the border between Monschau and Wasserbillig and no main highway crossing the forest from east to west. Everything went through a few crossroads: St. Vith and Malmedy in the north, Bastogne and Houffalize in the south. And at each of them there were, or soon would be, American troops.

The same brochure writes the sentence that sums up the game: if the Americans fought hard and well, the same terrain that guaranteed surprise would become a trap. On a board with few squares to pass through, every crossroads held stopped an entire column behind it.

Move 316 to 18 December: the northern shoulder does not give

On 16 December more than 200,000 Germans, in 13 infantry and 7 armored divisions, attacked a sector that the American 1st Army was holding with four divisions over about 142 km. The surprise was real: the weather had hidden the buildup, and Allied decryption of German messages had given hints, but no definitive statement of Hitler's intentions.

The main effort was Sepp Dietrich's 6th Panzer Army, in the north, with the shortest route to Antwerp. It needed the Elsenborn Ridge, which controlled access to two of its advance roads. The green 99th Division and the veteran 2nd held the ridge, reinforced by the 1st and the 9th. It was the only American sector attacked where the Germans did not advance. The strongest move of the plan jammed in the very first days.

In the center, Manteuffel's 5th Panzer Army had more success: two regiments of the 106th Division were encircled on the Schnee Eifel and surrendered on 19 December. But the 110th Regiment, in front of Bastogne, lost more than 80% of its men and delayed the German armored corps by 48 hours. And Eisenhower acted fast: he sent two armored divisions into the breach on the first day and the theater reserve on the second. Danny Parker writes that by 18 December Rundstedt already considered the offensive a failure.

Move 4St. Vith and Bastogne: the squares nobody can go around

The German plan called for taking St. Vith by 18:00 on 17 December. The 7th Armored Division, with CCB of the 9th Armored and remnants of the 106th and the 28th, held the town until the night of the 21st — four days beyond schedule. Montgomery authorized the withdrawal saying they came back with all honor, and on 23 December the defenders crossed the Salm. The U.S. Army brochure says the defense bought five critical days; Manteuffel admitted after the war that it upset the timetable of both panzer armies.

Bastogne was the other knot. The 101st Airborne arrived on the morning of 19 December, with CCB of the 10th Armored. On the 21st the last road was cut and the town was encircled. At noon on the 22nd came the German ultimatum, and General Anthony McAuliffe answered with one word: Nuts!

The 2nd Panzer Division went around the town and pushed on toward the Meuse. But an encircled town that does not fall still owns its roads, and the 5th Panzer Army had to divert armor to it instead of sending it forward. It is the same principle as St. Vith: on the Ardennes board, whoever held the crossroads held the opponent's flow.

Move 5The fuel ran out where the road ran out

The explanation that circulates most is the lack of gasoline, and it has facts on its side. According to Cole, the Germans had delivered 4,680,000 gallons by 16 December, but perhaps half was still on the Rhine bank; in the vehicles there was enough for 90 to 100 miles. The plan counted on capturing American fuel.

The northern spearhead, Joachim Peiper's column, refueled with American fuel at Büllingen on the second day, but did not reach the large depots north of Stavelot and Malmedy, with more than two million gallons. An American major burned 124,000 gallons in a ditch to block the road. Bridges were blown in front of the column, and on the night of 23 to 24 December Peiper left La Gleize on foot, with about 800 men, leaving behind 28 tanks, 70 half-tracks and 25 guns.

In the center, the 2nd Panzer Division reached Foy-Notre-Dame, four or five miles from the Dinant bridges, and ran dry there. Model reported that he had ordered the vanguard to go on foot to the Meuse. Between 25 and 26 December the American 2nd Armored Division destroyed the German spearhead near Celles.

But Cole himself concludes that the German problem was one of transport, not a general shortage of fuel: the gasoline existed, and did not reach the front because the roads were denied and bombed. Danny Parker disagrees on the weight: with the stock at the start, the Germans might have reached the Meuse, but they certainly would not have crossed it, and Antwerp was impossible. The two readings agree on what matters for the board: the fuel at the front was not enough, and the rest depended on roads the Americans did not hand over.

Move 6Patton's turn and the end of the cloud

On 19 December, at Verdun, Eisenhower opened the meeting saying the situation should be seen as an opportunity, not a disaster. Patton's 3rd Army, which was preparing to attack eastward, would turn north. Carlo D'Este records that Patton offered to attack on the morning of the 21st, with three divisions, and Eisenhower set the 22nd. III Corps — 4th Armored, 26th and 80th Infantry — attacked at 6:00 on 22 December. North of the breach, Montgomery had been in command since noon on the 20th.

The turn was fast, but it was no walkover: the attack set off 12 or 15 miles south of Bastogne, without surprise, and became a frontal assault against infantry on good ground. The 4th Armored only broke through by way of Assenois late in the afternoon of 26 December.

Bastogne held out until then because the sky had cleared. On 23 December cold, dry winds from the east, in Cole's words, stripped the German armies of their immunity to air attack. The week of bad weather promised to Hitler was over. Supply drops over Bastogne began, and Allied aircraft went on to fly an average of 3,000 sorties a day in good weather. But the clear sky caught a timetable that was already broken: the Meuse, planned for the third or fourth day, was never reached.

VerdictWhy it ended the way it did

The 1944 move was the same as in 1940, and it failed because the board was not. In 1940 the forest was practically empty, the weather was good and the opponent was looking the other way; even so, the crossing took three days. In 1944 there were Americans on both shoulders of the breach and at the crossroads, and every crossroads held — Elsenborn, St. Vith, Bastogne — stopped an entire column on terrain with few roads.

The fuel ran out, but it ran out where the road was denied and where the American depots did not fall. The sky cleared on 23 December, but it cleared over columns that were already late. And the opponent reacted within days: two armored divisions on the first, the reserve on the second, the 3rd Army turned north on the seventh.

In chess terms, it is repeating an opening that worked against a distracted player in front of one who now defends the central squares. The move is the same; the position is not. And the target was too far to be reached even if everything went right — the very field marshals who carried out the plan said so before it began.

MythsWhat is told and what the record shows

  • What is told

    The Germans lost because they ran out of gasoline.

    What the record shows

    The spearheads ran dry — Peiper at La Gleize, the 2nd Panzer Division a few kilometers from Dinant. But the U.S. Army's official history concludes that the problem was one of transport, not of general shortage: half the fuel delivered was still on the Rhine bank, and the American depots the plan counted on capturing did not fall. Danny Parker disagrees on the weight: even with everything, the Germans would not have got past the Meuse. The fuel ran out where the roads were denied.

  • What is told

    Patton turned his army in 48 hours and saved Bastogne single-handedly.

    What the record shows

    The Verdun meeting was on 19 December, and the three-division attack set off at 6:00 on the 22nd. Patton offered to attack on the 21st, and Eisenhower set the 22nd. The attack became a frontal assault and took until late in the afternoon of 26 December to reach Bastogne — which held out until then because it had been receiving supplies by air since the 23rd.

  • What is told

    Bad weather is what worked for the Germans, and good weather finished them.

    What the record shows

    The sky cleared on 23 December. By then the northern shoulder had jammed, St. Vith had cost four days beyond the plan and the Meuse, planned for the third or fourth day, had not been reached. Good weather finished off the offensive; it was not what delayed it.

  • What is told

    It was a repeat of 1940, and all that was missing was luck.

    What the record shows

    The American official sources themselves list the differences: in 1940 the defense was a light screen, the weather was good and the roads dry, and the crossing took three days. In 1944 there were divisions on the shoulders and at the crossroads, reserves arriving within hours, and a target that Model and Rundstedt considered unreachable before the attack began.

QuestionsFrequently asked questions

Who won the Battle of the Bulge?

The Allies won. The German offensive took no bridge on the Meuse, and in January 1945 the original line was restored. It was the last major German offensive in the West.

Why did Germany lose the Battle of the Bulge?

It repeated the 1940 move on a different board: on terrain with few roads, the Americans held the crossroads of Elsenborn, St. Vith and Bastogne, and each one stopped an entire column. The fuel did not reach the front, the sky cleared on 23 December and Antwerp was a target too far away.

Where was the Battle of the Bulge fought?

In the Ardennes forest, in Belgium and Luxembourg, from Monschau and the Elsenborn Ridge, in the north, to Echternach, in the south. The westernmost point of the German advance was near Celles and Dinant, less than 10 km from the Meuse River.

How many soldiers died in the Battle of the Bulge?

The Americans had 80,987 casualties; the dead range from about 8,400 to more than 19,000, depending on the period and the basis of the count. There is no official German count, and estimates range from about 63,000 to 104,000 casualties. About 3,000 civilians died.

How long did the Battle of the Bulge last?

From 16 December 1944 to 25 January 1945. The German offensive proper ran out of steam before the Meuse between 24 and 26 December; the rest was the Allied recapture of the lost ground.
