Episode 18 · Paraguayan War, 1865

Riachuelo

11 June 1865 · The Paraná River off the mouth of the Riachuelo stream, about 15 km below the city of Corrientes, Corrientes Province, Argentina

How a narrow river became a board: why the Paraguayan surprise attack failed, how Barroso turned the battle with a ram, and what the victory really closed off?

In June 1865 the Paraguayan army occupies Corrientes, in Argentina, and a Brazilian squadron of 9 steamers is anchored in the Paraná River, just below the city, to cut its line of communication. Francisco Solano López decides to take it: arrive in the dark, pass the anchored ships, turn and board each one before it could weigh anchor. The Paraguayan steamers were almost all armed merchantmen; in an artillery duel, they would lose.

This page opens the miniseries on the Paraguayan War — or the War of the Triple Alliance, as the neighboring countries call it — and follows the game of 11 June 1865: a plan that depended on the clock, a squadron that went in column into a narrow channel under a battery it had not seen, and a frigate used as a ram. The question is how the river became a board, and what the Brazilian victory actually closed off — which is less than is usually said.

Move 1The board: a seagoing squadron in a river channel

In April 1865 Paraguay invades the Argentine province of Corrientes and occupies its capital. The column moving down the bank of the Paraná depends on the river for supply, and the river is precisely what the Empire of Brazil can close. After supporting the Argentine attack on Corrientes of 25 May, Francisco Manuel Barroso's squadron lies at anchor on the Chaco side, a few kilometers below the city: 9 war steamers, 59 guns, about 2,300 men.

The position had strength and it had a flaw. It blocked the river, but it lay in territory the enemy controlled, between Corrientes to the north and the bluffs of the Riachuelo to the south. Admiral Francisco Eduardo Alves de Almeida, of the Brazilian Naval War College, puts it this way: the Brazilian force was not in a blockading position, but a blockaded one. Coal was short, and since 3 June the ships had been burning wood from the Chaco to save it for combat.

The board is the river itself. Below Corrientes the Paraná divides among islands and sandbanks, and the main channel runs hard against the bluff on the Corrientes bank — deep, but narrow and tricky, in the words of a pilot of the time. The Brazilian ships had been built for the sea, with deep draft. On the Paraguayan side, the only warship was the Tacuarí; the others were merchantmen with guns, with engines and boilers above the waterline. In an artillery duel, the outcome was settled. The plan had to be something else.

Move 2López's plan depended on the clock

The plan is told the same way by George Thompson, a British engineer in the Paraguayan army, by Juan Crisóstomo Centurión, who fought on the bluff, and by the Paraguayan officers heard after the war: be upon the Brazilians at daybreak, pass the anchored squadron, turn and bring each steamer alongside an imperial ship, fire a broadside and board, before they could weigh anchor. The Paraguayan sailors recommended attacking between 3 and 4 in the morning. On the bank, José María Bruguez took 22 field pieces to the Riachuelo bluff on the night of 10 June, to hit whoever fled downriver.

Everything depended on arriving in the dark, and everything ran late. The squadron left Humaitá after midnight, for a trip of about 7 hours. It took 6 barges — unpowered lighters, riding low in the water, each with one heavy gun —, included at the suggestion of a foreign colonel, against the opinion of the sailors, according to the memoirs of the officer Pedro Gill. Towing them forced a slow pace. And the steamer Yberá lost the key of its propeller on the way and fell behind. The second in command, Remigio Cabral, later pointed to the late departure as the main cause of the outcome.

The Paraguayans appeared at around nine in the morning on a Sunday with a clear sky. In the Brazilian squadron, the flag ceremony had already taken place and the lunch table was being laid. The commander, Pedro Ignacio Meza, went ahead. Centurión wrote that the prudent thing would have been to postpone. Without darkness, the boarding plan turned into the artillery duel it existed to avoid.

Move 3Meza chooses the square: the Riachuelo bluff

The Paraguayan line went down the channel along the Corrientes bank, about a mile from the Brazilian ships, exchanging fire for a few minutes. The Jejuí had its boiler pierced and dropped out of the fight. Thompson called the maneuver foolish: it gave the Brazilians the time to weigh anchor that the plan meant to deny them.

But Meza did not turn back to board in midstream. He anchored the line along the Riachuelo bluff, under Bruguez's guns and the infantry hidden in the brush, with the barges — which could only fire when stationary. Surprise lost, he set up a defense: if the Brazilians came down after him, they would fight in the narrow channel, under the bluff. The commander of the gunboat Araguari, Antônio Luís von Hoonholtz, who was on the other side, thought he had fallen into a carefully prepared ambush.

Move 4The imperial column goes in under the battery

Barroso ordered the attack and the squadron went down in column through the same channel, hard against the bluff, with the Belmonte in the lead and the flagship Amazonas falling behind. That is when the signal went up that the Brazilian Navy repeats to this day: “Brazil expects that each one will do his duty” (our translation) — an avowed allusion to Nelson's at Trafalgar. The Brazilians had not seen the battery. When the Belmonte drew near the Riachuelo, the bluff opened fire with cannon, musketry and rockets.

Under fire, the Jequitinhonha turned to go upriver, the ships behind followed her, and the Belmonte went on alone and took the fire of all of them. At around ten in the morning, taking on water, she ran herself aground on an island on purpose so as not to sink. The Jequitinhonha ran aground off the bluff and stayed there, under musketry and two guns brought up to close range. In the report he wrote the next day, Barroso noted the narrowness of the channel and the difficulties of the confused signal regulations. Almeida, himself a Navy officer, concludes that Barroso lost control of his force's movements at the start of the battle.

There is one consolation for the Brazilians, according to Hoonholtz and the Paraguayan major Julián Godoy: many shots from the bluff passed over the ships and fell on the island opposite. After about two hours, the Amazonas came down between the two lines, Barroso ordered by speaking trumpet that the others follow him, and the squadron rounded the Santa Catalina point, turned in a wider stretch and began to come back upriver.

Move 5The boarding that almost worked: the Parnaíba

With her rudder damaged and separated from the group, the gunboat Parnaíba was the ship the Paraguayan plan wanted. Meza sent the Tacuarí, the Salto Oriental and the Marqués de Olinda against her. The Tacuarí came alongside, but the paddle wheel pushed the hull away and only the men on top of the paddle box managed to jump; they went back, because the ships were not made fast to each other. Thompson notes the detail that explains a great deal: through an oversight, no Paraguayan steamer carried grappling hooks.

The Salto Oriental, a screw steamer, truly came alongside. Some 30 Paraguayans of the 6th Battalion jumped aboard, took the ship from the stern to the mainmast and hauled down the imperial flag. Captain Pedro Afonso Ferreira, of the Army's 9th Battalion, and Midshipman João Guilherme Greenhalgh died in the defense. It was the battle López had planned, fought on a single ship, hours late. The squadron coming back upriver swept the deck with grapeshot, and the crew retook the Parnaíba.

Move 6The ram: the frigate against the merchantmen

The move that turned the game was using the heaviest piece as a battering piece. The frigate Amazonas, the largest hull in the battle, went at the Paraguayan steamers bow first. Barroso consulted the Corrientes pilot Bernardino Gustavino about the water — in a narrow channel, running aground meant losing the ship — and, with an affirmative answer, rammed the first. Success led him to repeat it. Which ships and in what order the sources do not settle: Barroso speaks of four; the Argentine Pablo Palermo, cross-checking the Brazilian reports with Centurión, gives the Jejuí, the Salto Oriental, the Marqués de Olinda and the Paraguarí, plus one barge. The result does not differ: four Paraguayan steamers lost.

At around 16:00 the Tacuarí, the Igurey, the Yporá and the Pirabebé, under Cabral's command — Meza was gravely wounded —, went upriver. The gunboat Araguari pursued them until close to nightfall and was called back. All the barges were lost. The four steamers that returned to Humaitá had all their guns dismounted.

Barroso was criticized for not pursuing to the end. Thompson wrote that in another country he would have gone before a court-martial. Barroso himself explained that he preferred to destroy the squadron there rather than go up and down a channel where, sooner or later, he would run aground — and he had damaged ships, wounded men, enemy banks and night coming on. Almeida calls the decision questionable, taken in the heat of action. It is a choice with a cost, neither an obvious mistake nor an obvious success.

VerdictWhy it ended the way it did

The Paraguayan plan was the right one for the material López had: with armed merchantmen against warships, only a surprise boarding could even the game. It died on the clock — late departure, barges in tow, a broken-down steamer — and in Meza's decision to attack by day anyway. Without darkness and without grappling hooks, the boarding happened only once, on the Parnaíba, and it almost sufficed. On the other side, the imperial squadron made the classic mistake of the player with more pieces: it went in single file into a narrow corridor, under a battery it had not seen, and paid with the Jequitinhonha. The same commander who got the entry wrong repaired the game with the move only he could make: using the largest hull on the river as a ram.

In chess terms, it is the player who prepared a surprise attack and reaches the table with the clock already running against him, insists on the line, and ends up trading the pieces he could not afford to trade. Riachuelo took from Paraguay the only piece capable of contesting the river. What it closed off was the way out to the sea: from then on, nothing reached Paraguay by the Paraná. The river itself did not change hands that day — the imperial squadron fell back south, Paraguayan steamers still sailed between Asunción and Corrientes, and the Paraguay River was only mastered in 1868. Whoever dominates the banks is master of the river, Almeida wrote. The banks are the next game.

MythsWhat is told and what the record shows

  • What is told

    The Brazilians were caught asleep.

    What the record shows

    It was Sunday, the 8:00 flag ceremony had already taken place and the lunch table was being laid when the lookout on the gunboat Mearim gave the alarm, according to Barroso's report. The Paraguayan surprise failed because it arrived in broad daylight, not because it found anyone asleep.

  • What is told

    Barroso hid and it was the pilot Gustavino who commanded.

    What the record shows

    The story comes from George Thompson, the American minister Charles Washburn and the Englishman H. W. Wilson — none of the three was on the river, and Thompson writes that it is what was being said. The reports of five Brazilian commanders put Barroso on the bridge giving orders; in an 1877 inquiry, Gustavino testified that the first ramming was on Barroso's initiative and order, after consulting him about the depth. The Argentine historian Pablo Palermo concludes there is no evidence of cowardice.

  • What is told

    It was a clean victory, carried out as planned.

    What the record shows

    Barroso himself noted the narrow channel and the confused signal regulations. The column went in under a battery it had not seen, lost the corvette Jequitinhonha, almost lost the Belmonte and had the Parnaíba boarded with her flag hauled down. Almeida, a Navy officer: Barroso lost control at the start and regained it in the ramming phase.

  • What is told

    Riachuelo gave Brazil command of the rivers.

    What the record shows

    It gave the blockade: Paraguay received nothing more by river until the end of the war, and its squadron never again contested the Paraná. But the imperial squadron fell back south soon afterward, Paraguayan steamers kept sailing between Asunción and Corrientes, and the Paraguay River only passed to Allied control in 1868, with the fall of the fortresses, according to Francisco Doratioto.

  • What is told

    Paraguay simply lied when it said it had won.

    What the record shows

    The official press in Asunción proclaimed victory, adding to 11 June the 13th, when Brazilian gunboats gave up trying to refloat the Jequitinhonha under the batteries, and López awarded a medal with the legend Riachuelo, 11 y 13 de junio 1865. It was propaganda under government control, but the military result is not in dispute: the same newspaper acknowledged the loss of four steamers.

QuestionsFrequently asked questions

Who won the Battle of Riachuelo?

Brazil won. The Paraguayan squadron ceased to exist as a fighting force: it lost 4 steamers and all 6 barges, while Brazil lost one ship, the corvette Jequitinhonha, run aground and abandoned.

When and where was the Battle of Riachuelo?

On 11 June 1865, in the Paraguayan War, on the Paraná River off the mouth of the Riachuelo stream, about 15 km below the city of Corrientes, in Argentina.

Who commanded at the Battle of Riachuelo?

On the Brazilian side, Commodore Francisco Manuel Barroso da Silva, aboard the frigate Amazonas. On the Paraguayan side, Captain Pedro Ignacio Meza, aboard the Tacuarí, carrying out Francisco Solano López's order to attack; on land, the battery was Lieutenant Colonel José María Bruguez's.

How many died in the Battle of Riachuelo?

On the Brazilian side, the counts range from 87 to 116 dead; the sum of the combat reports gives 102. The Paraguayan government did not publish a list: Garmendia estimates about 750 casualties in the squadron and 30 in the battery, and George Thompson speaks of about 200.

Why was the Battle of Riachuelo important?

It took from Paraguay the only force capable of contesting the river: from then on, nothing reached the country by the Paraná until the end of the war. But it did not give Brazil command of the rivers, because the imperial squadron fell back south and the Paraguay River only passed to Allied control in 1868.
