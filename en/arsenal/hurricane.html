Hawker Hurricane

single-seat fighter · 1937–1945

The Hurricane was the RAF's first monoplane fighter with eight machine guns, in service since December 1937. In 1940 it was slower than the Spitfire and than the Bf 109, and had half the fame.

It also had what fame does not show: it was cheaper to build, easier to repair and was in more squadrons. This page covers the Mk I, the version that flew in the Battle of Britain.

On the board 1What it was for

The Hurricane was the fighter for the defense of British territory, made to climb, catch bombers and shoot them down with eight machine guns. When the war began, it was the backbone of Fighter Command, and it was the one the RAF sent to France in 1940.

In the Battle of Britain, the ideal division of labor, according to the Smithsonian and John Correll, was this: the Hurricane squadrons went for the bombers, and the faster Spitfires held off the escort. That was the ideal. Correll points out that the Hurricanes also shot down their share of the fighters.

On the board 2Strengths

The first is the factory. By the official British history of war production, by M. M. Postan, a Hurricane airframe cost 10,300 hours of work in the estimate of January 1940, against 15,200 for a Spitfire. In Richard Overy's tables, from June to November 1940 there came out 1,367 Hurricanes and 724 Spitfires.

The second is the workshop. The steel-tube structure stood up to damage, and, according to the Smithsonian, let many aircraft survive combat. The Battle of Britain Memorial Trust records that it was not rare for a damaged Hurricane to be back in action the same day.

The third is the shooting. The four machine guns in each wing sat close together, which concentrated the fire, and the aircraft was steady when firing. And it turned well: for the Smithsonian, the Hurricane turned tighter than the German fighter at all operating altitudes.

On the board 3Weak points

It was slower. The Smithsonian writes that the Bf 109 outdid the Hurricane in speed and in armament; Stephen Bungay, as cited by the English Wikipedia, gives a difference of 10 to 30 miles per hour depending on altitude, and adds that, in a tail chase, the 109 got away.

The engine was the same Merlin as the Spitfire's, with the same defect: the float carburetor cut the fuel supply when the pilot suddenly pushed the nose down.

And there was fire. A fuel tank sat in the fuselage, just ahead of the instrument panel, and many Hurricane pilots suffered severe burns. Geoffrey Page, one of them, compared the experience to sitting in front of a giant blowtorch. Dowding had the tanks lined with a self-sealing material.

VerdictWhat the piece was worth on the board

On the board, the Hurricane is the common piece that decides by being everywhere. It was not the best piece on the British side, and nobody at the time said it was. It was the one that existed in quantity, the one that came back from the workshop and the one the factory replaced.

In a battle of attrition like that of 1940, in which the question was how many fighters each side could keep in the air, those three things were worth more than 30 miles per hour.

MythsWhat is told and what the record shows

  • What is told

    The Spitfire won the Battle of Britain.

    What the record shows

    The Hurricane was more numerous and took most of the victories claimed by Fighter Command: 55% according to BAE Systems, against 42% for the Spitfire; more than 60% according to the RAF Museum. These are claims, which both sides inflated, not confirmed German losses.

  • What is told

    The Hurricane was an aircraft of wood and canvas.

    What the record shows

    The structure was a truss of steel tubes. Wood and linen gave only the shape and the covering of the rear part of the fuselage, and the wings, fabric-covered on the first Mk Is, became metal in 1939.

  • What is told

    The Hurricanes attacked the bombers and the Spitfires took care of the fighters.

    What the record shows

    That was the ideal division, described by the Smithsonian and by John Correll. Vincent Orange, as cited by the English Wikipedia, writes that the ideal was not always achieved and that there were occasions when the roles were reversed.

  • What is told

    The Hurricane of 1940 reached 340 miles per hour.

    What the record shows

    That is the figure for the Mk IIC, with a more powerful engine, at BAE Systems. The official trials of the Mk I gave 320 miles per hour in 1938 and 316 in 1940.

QuestionsFrequently asked questions

How many Hurricanes were built?

About 14,500. The sources differ: 14,233 at the Smithsonian, about 14,483 at BAE Systems, 14,487 in Owen Thetford and 14,533 at the Royal Air Force.

Was the Hurricane better than the Spitfire?

Not in performance: it was slower. It was cheaper to build, with 10,300 hours of work per airframe against 15,200, easier to repair and more numerous.

What was the Hurricane's armament?

On the Mk I, eight Browning machine guns of .303 inch, four in each wing, grouped together.

What was the Hurricane's maximum speed?

The sources differ. The official British trials of the Mk I gave 320 miles per hour in 1938 and 316 in 1940; the Canadian Air Force gives 318, about 512 km/h.
